# The Longest and Shortest Sugyot in the Talmud Yerushalmi

Anyone who has spent time learning the Yerushalmi knows it's a very different beast from the Bavli — terser, more elliptical, with sugyot that can disappear before you've settled into your chair. But just how lopsided is it? We crunched the numbers across all 39 tractates of the Jerusalem Talmud as it appears on ChavrutAI, and the results are striking.

## The numbers at a glance

The Yerushalmi, as we have it, contains **2,162 sugyot** (halakhot) totaling roughly **809,000 Hebrew words**. The average sugya runs about 374 words; the median is just 260. But those averages hide an enormous range: the longest sugya is **201 times** the length of the shortest.

## The shortest sugyot

The runt of the litter is **Sotah 9.8**, which weighs in at a grand total of 24 Hebrew words — and that's counting both the Mishnah and the gemara. The full Talmudic discussion is just two words: *bera ketola* ("a son of murderers"), a brief gloss on the mishnah's report that Elazar ben Dinai was nicknamed "the murderer's son."

Here are the ten shortest sugyot in the entire Yerushalmi:

| # | Sugya | Hebrew words |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 | Sotah 9:8 | 24 |
| 2 | Beitzah 2:9 | 28 |
| 3 | Nedarim 7:4 | 29 |
| 4 | Rosh Hashanah 4:5 | 32 |
| 5 | Eruvin 7:7 | 33 |
| 6 | Nedarim 6:10 | 34 |
| 7 | Avodah Zarah 5:2 | 34 |
| 8 | Yevamot 13:9 | 35 |
| 9 | Yevamot 13:10 | 35 |
| 10 | Nedarim 7:9 | 35 |

Notice how Nedarim shows up three times in the top ten. The Yerushalmi to Nedarim is famously laconic — sometimes a single line of commentary on a long Mishnah.

## The longest sugyot

At the other extreme, a handful of sugyot sprawl out as full-length essays — the kind of multi-page discussion the Bavli is famous for. The reigning champion is **Shabbat 7:2**, the great catalogue of the 39 *avot melachot* (categories of forbidden Sabbath labor), at nearly **5,000 Hebrew words**.

| # | Sugya | Hebrew words |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 | Shabbat 7:2 | 4,816 |
| 2 | Peah 1:1 | 4,503 |
| 3 | Sanhedrin 10:2 | 3,881 |
| 4 | Moed Katan 3:5 | 3,390 |
| 5 | Taanit 4:5 | 3,118 |
| 6 | Sanhedrin 1:2 | 3,046 |
| 7 | Kiddushin 1:2 | 2,858 |
| 8 | Berakhot 1:1 | 2,806 |
| 9 | Yoma 1:1 | 2,790 |
| 10 | Chagigah 2:1 | 2,729 |

The list reads like a greatest-hits album of the Yerushalmi. Peah 1:1 is the celebrated discussion of mitzvot whose fruits are eaten in this world while the principal is preserved for the next. Taanit 4:5 contains the haunting Yerushalmi narratives of the destruction of Beitar and the death of Bar Kokhba. Sanhedrin 10:2 dives deep into who has — and doesn't have — a share in the world to come. Several of the openings of tractates also make the list (Berakhot 1:1, Yoma 1:1, Kiddushin 1:2, Sanhedrin 1:2): the Yerushalmi tends to begin tractates with extended methodological and theological framing before settling into a quicker pace.

## What's missing entirely

Here's something many learners don't realize: the Yerushalmi we have today is **incomplete**. Substantial portions never made it down to us, and a few stretches were apparently never composed. On ChavrutAI, after careful checking against Sefaria's text, we identified **49 mishnayot** that have no Yerushalmi gemara at all. We've now hidden these from navigation and search so you won't bump into empty pages, but it's worth knowing where the gaps are:

- **Shabbat, chapters 21–24** — the entire final four chapters of Yerushalmi Shabbat are lost. The text simply ends after chapter 20.
- **Makkot, chapter 3** — the entire third (and final) chapter has no gemara.
- **Niddah, from 4:2 onward** — the Yerushalmi to Niddah is itself only four chapters long, and from the second halakhah of chapter 4 onward there is no Talmudic discussion. (Sefaria preserves Moïse Schwab's 19th-century French translation of the bare mishnayot for these sections, but Heinrich Guggenheimer — whose English translation underlies most modern study — only translated Mishnah passages that have accompanying gemara.)
- **Ketubot 4:5** — a single isolated gap.
- **Yevamot** — ten scattered halakhot lack gemara: 2:5, 4:5, 4:6, 4:14, 5:5, 5:7, 7:2, 8:4, 9:6, and 9:7.

These gaps are not the result of poor digitization; they reflect the actual textual situation of the Yerushalmi in every manuscript we have. The Bavli, by contrast, is essentially complete on the tractates it covers (though it covers fewer tractates of Seder Zeraim and Toharot — the inverse problem).

## Why it matters

The dramatic spread between Sotah 9:8's two-word gemara and Shabbat 7:2's near-5,000-word treatise is more than a curiosity. It tells you something about how the Yerushalmi was edited: less rounded off than the Bavli, more willing to leave a passing remark *as* a passing remark, and unwilling to manufacture discussion where the editors had nothing substantive to add. When the Yerushalmi has something to say — about creation, about Bar Kokhba, about the world to come — it can rival anything in the Bavli for depth. When it doesn't, it stays quiet. That asymmetry is part of what makes learning Yerushalmi feel different. You're closer to the rough surface of the editorial work, with all its gaps and silences, than you are in the smoother prose of the Bavli.

So the next time you sit down with a Yerushalmi sugya and finish it in thirty seconds, you're not missing something — that *is* the sugya. And the next time one swallows up a full afternoon, know that you're in distinguished company: those long sugyot are exactly where the Yerushalmi has chosen to plant its flags.
